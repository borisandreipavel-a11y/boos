# The screen: painting, letters, the panel, the table, the kernel's lines and the look

Code: `src/screen.rs`, `src/letters.rs` for the shapes of the letters, `src/lines.rs` for the kernel's own lines, `src/drawing.rs` (with `src/drawing_values.rs`) for a table the model describes, and `src/look.rs` for the looks. The chat box, which is drawn with these pieces, has its own file and note: `src/chat.rs` and [chat.md](chat.md). The question to the firmware, `screen_find`, and the `Screen` it gives are in `src/firmware.rs`; [startup.md](startup.md) covers them.

## What the screen is

- A picture on the screen is a grid of dots (pixels). The dot at the top left is column 0 of row 0. Columns count to the right, rows count downwards.
- The screen's memory (the framebuffer) holds four bytes for every dot, row after row, starting with the top row. Three of the bytes say how much red, green and blue the dot shows, each from 0 to 255. The fourth is not used.
- Writing into that memory changes the picture at once. There is no "show it now" step and no second copy of the picture.
- The dot at column x of row y sits at `memory_start + (y * row_length + x) * 4`. `row_length` is how many dots a row takes up in the memory, which can be more than the picture is wide. Counting rows in `width` instead puts every row after the first in the wrong place on such a screen.
- Two orders of the three colours are known: red, green, blue and blue, green, red. `Screen.colours` says which one this screen uses.

## The pieces

- **`Colour`** is three amounts, `red`, `green` and `blue`, each a byte.
- **`screen_size()`** says how many dots the screen is across and down, or nothing on a machine without a usable screen. The chat box, the frame of a table and the kernel's lines work out their place from it.
- **`screen_setup(screen)`** takes the `Screen` that `screen_find` gave and keeps it in `SCREEN`. It is called once from `efi_main`, right after the takeover, so that the kernel's lines can be shown from then on (see "The kernel's lines" below). Until the kernel's own page tables are in place, the firmware's lead to the screen's memory: the firmware wrote its own text there a moment before. On a machine without a usable screen it is not called, `SCREEN` stays `None`, and the drawing functions do nothing.
- **`screen_reach()`** makes the screen's memory reachable through the kernel's own page tables with `map_device_memory`. It is called right after `page_tables_setup`, before anything is painted again: the screen's memory may lie above the 4 GB those tables cover. On QEMU's display cards it lies below, and nothing needs to be added.
- **`dot_value(order, colour)`** turns a colour into the number that is written for a dot. A dot is written as one number of four bytes. The lowest byte of a number is the one that lands first in memory, so the colour the screen wants first goes into the lowest byte. The fourth byte stays 0.
- **`screen_rectangle(left, top, width, height, colour)`** paints a filled rectangle, one dot at a time with `device_write_32`. While the kernel's lines cover the screen it paints nothing, and neither does `screen_text`; `screen_rectangle_over` and `screen_text_over` paint all the same, for the kernel's lines (see "The kernel's lines" below). `screen_cover` says whether the lines cover the screen, and `screen_covered` gives the answer back.
- **`screen_fill(colour)`** paints the whole screen: a rectangle from the top left corner, as wide and as high as the screen.
- **`screen_text(left, top, text, size, colour)`** writes one line of text; see "Letters" below.
- **`screen_panel(text)`** shows a text in the box that apps show their texts in, and **`screen_panel_again()`** paints that box again with the text it showed last; see "The panel" below.
- **`screen_background()`** paints the background: the whole screen in one colour and the sentence near its top left corner, in the colours of the look in use; see "What a boot shows" and "The look" below.

## A rectangle is cut off at the screen's edges

- A rectangle that reaches past the right or bottom edge is cut off there. One that begins outside the screen paints nothing. So does one with a width or height of 0.
- This is what keeps drawing inside the screen's memory. Without it, dots past the right edge land at the start of the next row, and dots past the bottom edge land behind the last row: in memory of the display card that is not shown, in another device's controls, or in ordinary memory. Nothing would report that.
- The sums `left + width` and `top + height` stop at the largest number instead of wrapping round (`saturating_add`), so a huge width cannot turn into a small one.
- Any new drawing function must keep to the same rule: no address is written that was not first checked against `width` and `height`.

## Letters

The screen only knows dots, so every sign needs a small picture of itself. The pictures were drawn by hand for this kernel and are in `letters.rs`. No file was copied or downloaded for them. The shapes are not an invention, though: they follow the long-established way of writing letters on a grid of 5 by 7 dots, known from dot displays and old printers, as the one who drew them remembered it.

- A picture is 5 dots across and 9 down, written out in the source as nine rows of five signs: `#` for a dot that is painted, `.` for one that is left alone. The first seven rows hold the sign. The last two are for the tails of g, j, p, q and y, the comma, the semicolon and the underline, which hang below the line of writing.
- There are 95 pictures, `SHAPES`: the signs of plain English text in the order computers number them (ASCII), from the space, number 32, to `~`, number 126. `letter_shape(sign)` finds a sign's picture. Any other sign gets `UNKNOWN`, an empty box: letters with accents, a line break, a tab, and everything else.
- To change the look of a sign, change its picture. A row that is not 5 signs long, or a picture that is not 9 rows high, stops the build, because each row is a `[u8; 5]` and each picture nine of them. A sign other than `#` in a row counts as "left alone"; nothing checks for that.
- `screen_text` goes through the text sign by sign. For every `#` in a sign's picture it paints a square of `size` by `size` dots with `screen_rectangle`. With `size` 1 a letter is 5 by 9 dots of the screen, with 2 it is 10 by 18, and so on. The next sign starts 6 times `size` further right: the sign's width and one empty column.
- Only the dots of the letters are painted. What lies between and around them stays as it was, so text can be written over any background, and writing a second text on the same spot mixes the two.
- A text is one line. Nothing wraps round at the right edge and a line break is not understood. A text that reaches past the right or bottom edge is cut off there, in the middle of a sign if need be, because every square goes through `screen_rectangle`. The sums for a square's place stop at the largest number instead of wrapping round, so a huge `size` cannot put a dot in a wrong place.
- The next line of text belongs at least 9 times `size` lower; 10 times `size` leaves one empty row between the tails of one line and the tops of the next.
- With `size` 2, QEMU's usual screen of 1280 by 800 dots holds 106 signs across and 40 lines.
- How the letters look was judged by eye, in pictures of all 95 at several sizes; see [testing.md](testing.md).

## The panel

An app can ask the kernel to show a text on the screen (`kernel.show`, see [box.md](box.md)). The kernel shows it in a box. The code calls that box a panel, because "box" already means the locked box that apps run in.

- **The app says what, the kernel decides how.** An app hands over a text and nothing else. It cannot paint, and it cannot say where the panel goes or how it looks.
- **Its sizes are kept in one place,** `PANEL_STYLE` in `screen.rs`: the panel's width and height, the thickness of its frame, the size of the letters, how far the text begins from the left and top edges, and how far the panel stays below the panel of apps. Where the panel sits is decided next to them: 20 dots under the panel of apps, at its left edge, on every screen and whether or not any app is kept (`apps_panel_place` in `apps_kept.rs` says where the panel of apps is), so that it never covers the frame of a table beside the panel of apps. It sat in the middle of the screen until tables came. They are fixed when the kernel is built. **Its colours come from the look in use** (see "The look" below): the frame's, the inside's and the letters'. Changing how every app's text looks means changing these values or picking another look, and nothing else.
- **Its sizes today:** 520 by 60 dots, a frame 3 dots thick, letters at size 2, the text 20 dots from the left edge and 21 from the top. At that width and letter size one line of 40 signs fits, which is why an app's text may be 40 bytes long. In the first look the frame is orange, the inside light (240, 240, 240) and the letters black.
- **`screen_panel(text)`** paints the whole panel in the frame's colour, paints the inside over it, smaller by the frame's thickness on every side, and writes the text. Painting the whole panel each time wipes out what it showed before, so a short text after a long one leaves nothing of the long one. It remembers the signs it wrote, at most 160 bytes, which holds 40 signs of any kind, so that **`screen_panel_again()`** can paint the panel again with them in another look. Before any app has shown a text, `screen_panel_again` does nothing.
- **Nothing an app shows lands outside the panel.** `screen_text` stops only at the screen's edges, so `screen_panel` holds the text to the panel itself: it writes only as many signs as fit whole between the left and right edges, and writes nothing if a line of letters does not fit between top and bottom. This holds for any values in the style, and does not lean on the 40-byte limit in `app_box.rs`. On a screen smaller than the panel, the panel and its text are cut off at the screen's edges like everything else.
- **There is one panel for every app.** The app that shows last wins. What it shows stays after the app has ended. The kernel keeps the text it shows and nothing else about it, so that a change of look can paint the panel again.
- On a machine without a usable screen `screen_panel` does nothing.

## The table

Code: `src/drawing.rs`, and `src/drawing_values.rs`, made by `test-tools/make_drawing_values.py`. The model can describe a table in a few lines of text with the tool `draw_table` (see [tools.md](tools.md), where its text and how the kernel reads it stand), and the kernel draws it in a frame of its own.

- **Where the frame sits:** beside the panel of apps, 20 dots right of it, its top level with the panel's top, its right edge the chat box's right edge, and its bottom 20 dots above the chat box (`frame_place`). On QEMU's usual screen of 1280 by 800 dots it covers columns 660 to 1239 and rows 80 to 559, 580 by 480 dots; on a screen of 1024 by 768 it would be 324 by 448, worked out and not tried. It never lies over the chat box, the panel of apps or the box with an app's text. On a screen narrower than 1000 dots, on one without room for the head and a row, and on a machine without a usable screen there is no frame, and the tool is not offered. It is painted with the first table, and stays until the machine is switched off.
- **Its sizes are kept in one place,** `TABLE_STYLE` in `drawing.rs`: the gap of 20 dots, a frame 3 dots thick, 10 dots of room inside it, the chat box's letters (size 2, 10 by 18 dots), lines 2 dots thick, a cell's text 5 dots from the line on its left, 3 from the line on its right (a sign carries an empty column of 2 dots on its right, so it looks 5 from both) and 4 from the lines above and below, at most 6 columns, and 1000 dots as the narrowest screen.
- **Inside the frame:** the title at the top left of the room inside, in a row 28 dots high; below it the table, each row 28 dots high (the line above it, 4 dots, the letters, 4 dots), and a line below the last row. A column holds its signs at 12 dots each, plus 8 dots of room and a line of 2. On 1280 by 800 the room inside is 554 dots across and the title holds 46 signs; the head and 14 rows fit, or 15 rows without a head, and with 6 columns 41 signs fit across, all columns together (43 with 3).
- **The columns' widths,** in signs (`widths_worked_out`): a column wants as many signs as its longest cell, the head's among them, in the rows that are drawn, or as many as the model asked for it if that is more (only when it gave a width for every column), and at least one: a width asked is the least a column gets. If what the columns want fits across together, each gets it, and no cell is cut. If not, each column gets what it wants up to an equal share of the room, and the signs left over are shared among the columns that want more, in proportion to how many more each wants, rounded down; so a column of short cells stays whole beside one of long cells, which are cut. The table is as wide as its columns, from the left of the room inside. (At first the widths asked were taken as they were, and the room shared equally without them; a real model asked for a column one sign narrower than its own head, which was cut.)
- **Cells and titles** are cleaned byte by byte as every text from outside (each byte that is not a plain sign becomes a dot) and cut to their room, so that nothing of them lands outside the cell or the frame. A piece of the key in the description is starred first.
- **Rows that do not fit are left out,** and the kernel says how many (see [tools.md](tools.md)). So are columns past the sixth.
- **Painting:** the frame as the other boxes have it, the whole of it in the look's frame colour and its inside in the look's inside colour, which wipes out the table shown before; the title in the look's letters; then the whole table in the colour of its lines, and each cell over it in its background, leaving the lines showing between and round the cells; then each cell's text.
- **The colours** are the model's, picked to fit the wish, for five parts: the head's background and letters, the rows' background and letters, and the lines. A part it leaves out takes the look's: the head the frame's colour, the rows the inside's, letters the look's letters, the lines the frame's. The frame and the title are always the look's.
- **Letters that can be read.** The head's letters against the head's background, and the rows' letters against theirs, must stand out by a contrast ratio of at least 4.5, the measure the looks are held to (see "The look" below). If they do not, they are painted white, if white stands out more from the background than black, and black otherwise; one of the two always stands out by more than 4.5. This holds for the look's own colours too: in Night the head's light letters on its grey-blue are too faint (3.6) and become black. The lines and the backgrounds are not checked. The ratio is worked out in whole numbers (`readable`): each of a colour's three amounts of light, from 0 to 255, is made linear with `LINEAR`, a list of 256 values in millionths (the amount c = v / 255 made linear as WCAG 2 has it: c / 12.92 for c up to 0.04045, ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) to the power 2.4 above, rounded to the nearest millionth); the brightness is 2126, 7152 and 722 times the three, so that white is 10,000,000,000; and with 500,000,000 for the measure's 0.05, letters stand out when 2 × (lighter + 500,000,000) is at least 9 × (darker + 500,000,000), and white stands out more than black when (10,000,000,000 + 500,000,000) × 500,000,000 is more than (background + 500,000,000) squared. The list is made by a script, not by hand, because the power 2.4 is not whole-number arithmetic.
- **Kept in memory only:** the table drawn last, as it was drawn (its title, the colours the model picked, the widths, the head and the rows that fit, every cell cut to its column), is kept in `TABLE` until the machine is switched off, and a new table takes its place. `drawing_paint_again` paints it again whenever the whole screen is painted (`look_paint`): in another look, after the kernel's own lines went away (F1 and a key), after `setup`. The parts the model left out then take the new look's colours. Nothing of it is written to the disk, and the model is not told what the table holds after the line that drew it.
- **Only the startup code and the main loop** may call the functions of `drawing.rs`; `TABLE` is a plain `static mut` that nothing protects, taken out of its place and put back by value.

## The kernel's lines

Code: `src/lines.rs`. Everything the kernel says goes out through the serial port (see "Printing" in [startup.md](startup.md)), and many PCs have none. So every line is kept for the screen too, and shown there: on the whole screen while the machine starts, again when F1 is pressed, and with the reason when the kernel stops on a failure or a panic.

- **Every line is kept.** `serial_print` hands every text to `lines_keep` before it sends it, whether or not the machine has a serial port, and so does `print` in `firmware.rs`, which makes "Hello from my kernel!" the first line. A line ends at `\n`, a `\r` is left out, and a byte that is not a plain sign is kept as a sign without a picture, which shows as the empty box. The last 100 lines are kept, each with at most 300 signs (`LINES_KEPT`, `LINE_LONGEST`); the rest of a longer line is in the terminal only. They are kept in a room of fixed size, not on the heap: it works before the heap exists, and while the kernel stops on a failure that may have come in the middle of a change to the heap.
- **How they are laid out.** White letters at size 2, as large as the chat box's, 8 dots from the edges of the screen, on the dark blue (0, 40, 90) of the first look. A row is 20 dots high: a letter and one empty row of its dots. How many signs fit on a row and how many rows fit is worked out from the screen's size: 105 signs and 39 rows on QEMU's usual screen of 1280 by 800 dots, 65 signs and 29 rows on one of 800 by 600. A longer line goes on in the next row. When a line does not fit below the others, the older half goes at once: the screen is filled again, the newest lines that fill half of it are painted at the top, and the line goes below them.
- **Their colours are not part of the look.** The start is painted before the look kept on the disk is known, and nothing may make the report of a failure hard to read. White letters stand out from the dark blue by a contrast ratio of 14.5, and from the dark red of a stop by 9.9, by the measure the looks are held to (see "The look" below).
- **While the machine starts they cover the whole screen.** `efi_main` calls `lines_begin` right after the takeover, as soon as the screen is known: the firmware's text is painted over with the dark blue, the lines kept so far go at the top, and every line after them is painted as soon as it is printed. A failure from then on shows on the screen too (see "When the kernel stops" below).
- **The usual picture waits meanwhile.** While the lines cover the screen (`screen_cover`), `screen_rectangle` and `screen_text` paint nothing. The background, the chat box, the panel and the panel of apps go on keeping what they show, such as the chat box's rows, the text an app showed and the apps kept, and none of it reaches the screen. So the panel's first text at startup, "Hello from an app in the box", is never seen; the script's text takes its place before the usual picture is painted.
- **When the start is over** the usual picture takes the lines' place: the main loop prints the rest count, `lines_start_over` paints the whole usual picture at once with `look_paint`, in the look in use, which is the look kept in the notebook by then, and only then is the prompt printed. That takes about a quarter of a second, with device signals on: a task that prints meanwhile has its line come between the rest count and the prompt in the terminal. A key pressed before that, once the tasks run, ends the start at once, and is typed as usual.
- **F1 shows them again.** The main loop hands every key number to `lines_key` before it turns it into a letter. F1, key number 59, pressed while the usual picture shows, covers the screen with the newest lines that fit, and every line printed after it is added at the bottom, the older half moving off when the screen is full. Any key brings the usual picture back and is typed as usual; F1 itself types nothing. A key held down sends its number again and again until it is let go, so F1 counts once until it is let go or another key goes down: held down, it does not take the lines away again. What shows is everything the terminal gets: the lines that begin with "Kernel:" and the person's lines, an app's printed lines, and the lines of a script the model wrote. On a machine without a usable screen F1 does nothing.
- **Who paints them.** While the machine starts nothing runs but `efi_main`, and a line is painted as soon as it is whole, inside `serial_print`, with device signals off like the printing itself. Right before device signals are let through, `efi_main` calls `lines_tasks_start`: from then on other tasks print too, and only the main loop draws (see "Drawing from tasks" below), so a line is kept when it is printed and painted by the main loop at its next turn (`lines_paint_new`), at most a tick later.
- **On a machine without a usable screen** the lines are kept and shown nowhere; the start is as before.

### When the kernel stops

- `report_fault` (`faults.rs`) and the panic handler (`main.rs`) first call `lines_stopping`, which stops every other painting and keeps a line half put together as it is, so that the report starts a line of its own. Then they print the report as before, which is kept as the newest line, and then `lines_stop_screen` paints the stop screen: the whole screen dark red (140, 0, 0); at the top, in white at size 2, "The kernel stopped:" and the report, the same line the terminal gets; an empty row; "What the kernel said before that:"; and the newest lines before the report that fit, the oldest first.
- It works at any moment once the screen is known: during the start, with the usual picture on the screen, with the lines after F1, from any task. It uses no heap and paints only once: a failure inside the painting is reported through the serial port only, and then the processor stops.
- A failure before the screen is known, in the moment between the firmware stepping aside and `screen_setup`, or on a machine without a usable screen, shows nothing on the screen. So does a failure inside the report itself, or running out of stack, which reset the machine without a word (see [interrupts.md](interrupts.md)).
- The report is the newest line kept. A panic whose message holds a line break shows only the last piece of it there; the pieces before it stand at the end of the lines below.

## Drawing from tasks

- Nothing protects the screen against two tasks drawing at once. Each dot is written in one step and no state is shared, so the worst that can happen is a mixed picture: some dots from one task, some from the other. Nothing is damaged.
- `SCREEN` is written once, at startup, before any task runs. After that it is only read. So is `COVERED`, which says whether the kernel's lines cover the screen: the startup code and the main loop change it.
- At startup, before device signals are let through, only the kernel's lines are painted, by `efi_main` as they are printed; the usual picture waits (see "The kernel's lines" above).
- The chat box is drawn after startup, with device signals on: the main loop paints it again whenever a key is typed, and the whole screen when a look is tried (see "The look" below). So is the panel, when an app made on request runs and shows a text: it runs in the main loop too (see [apps.md](apps.md)). So are the kernel's lines that other tasks print, the whole usual picture when the start is over, and the lines after F1. The main loop is the only task that draws then, so nothing mixes. See [chat.md](chat.md).
- The stop screen is painted by whichever task fails, with device signals off, and nothing runs after it.
- The panel's remembered text is written by `screen_panel` and read by `screen_panel_again`, and nothing protects it either: both may be called at startup or from the main loop only.

## What a boot shows

Right after the takeover `efi_main` covers the screen with the kernel's lines: the firmware's own text is painted over with the dark blue (red 0, green 40, blue 90), "Hello from my kernel!" stands at the top left in white, and every line is added below as it is printed (see "The kernel's lines" above). The usual picture is made ready at the screen line, where `screen_background` and `chat_setup` are called, but it waits.

When the start is over, about four seconds after the devices start, the whole usual picture is painted at once, in the look kept in the notebook: the screen in the look's background colour, in the first look the dark blue, with one sentence in the look's colour for it, white in the first look, at size 2, its top left corner 40 dots from the left and 40 from the top: "Hello from my kernel! I draw my own letters now."; the chat box along the bottom ([chat.md](chat.md) says where it sits and how it looks); and under the place of the panel of apps the panel with "Hello from a Lua script!!!" in it, the text that a script, run by the interpreter in the box, asked to have shown at startup (see [interpreter.md](interpreter.md)). Before it, after the encryption line, an app in the box had the panel show "Hello from an app in the box", while the usual picture waited, so that text is not seen. On QEMU's usual screen of 1280 by 800 dots the panel covers columns 40 to 559 and rows 258 to 317, under the panel of apps, and its text begins at column 60, row 279.

The sentence is a demonstration and can go when something better shows that drawing works. An orange rectangle that used to be painted in the middle was such a demonstration and went when the panel came.

## How long it takes

Measured in QEMU, in the usual kind of build, by filling the screen ten times and counting timer ticks. The run had no window, and such runs can count too few ticks (see [testing.md](testing.md)), so the times may be up to half as much again; the ratio between them stands.

- Filling 1280 by 800 dots took 155 ticks for ten fills: roughly 0.16 s a fill, or 150 billionths of a second a dot. At startup that time is spent with device signals off.
- Writing a whole row with one call of Rust's own `fill` on a list of dots, instead of one `device_write_32` per dot, took 7 ticks for the same ten fills, about twenty times faster. It was tried and not kept, because one write per dot is the plainest thing to read. It is the first thing to change when drawing gets too slow.
- Writing the sentence of 48 signs at size 2 took 19 ticks for two hundred times, so about a thousandth of a second once. Every painted dot of a letter is one call of `screen_rectangle`.
- Showing a text in the panel was not timed by itself. An app that showed in a loop, with the limit on showing taken out and one printed line per showing, managed about 115 showings a second, so one is under a hundredth of a second.
- Painting the whole screen again in another look (`look_paint`) took 24 or 25 ticks, a quarter of a second, seven times in a row, in a windowless run; most of it is the fill.
- Showing the kernel's lines makes a start about a sixth of a second longer, by this computer's clock, in five windowless starts of each kernel taken in turns (`time_lines.py`), against the kernel before them: from "Hello from my kernel!" to the prompt 5.54 s (5.54 to 5.56) against 5.38 s (5.34 to 5.44). Filling the screen right after the takeover adds 0.16 s before the next line; holding the usual picture back while the machine starts saves about a quarter of a second until the devices start, so the lock's check comes 0.06 s sooner; and painting the usual picture whole at the end of the start adds 0.24 s between the rest count and the prompt, where nothing was before.

## The look

Code: `src/look.rs`. A look is the colour of everything the kernel draws: the background and the sentence; the chat box's frame, the colour behind what was said and behind the row being typed, and the person's and the kernel's letters; and the panel's frame, inside and letters, which the panel of apps and the frame of a table use too. Sizes and places are not part of a look. They stay in `CHAT_STYLE`, `PANEL_STYLE` and `TABLE_STYLE`, so every look fits every screen exactly as the first does.

- **There are four looks, `LOOKS`,** numbered from 1: Blue (the first, a dark blue screen with light boxes and orange frames), Night (an almost black screen, dark grey boxes with grey-blue frames, light letters), Paper (a white screen, white boxes with grey frames, black and dark brown letters) and Contrast (a black screen, black boxes with yellow frames, white and yellow letters). Their colours are written out in `look.rs`.
- **Every look is readable by a stated measure.** Letters stand out from what lies behind them by a contrast ratio of at least 4.5, and frames from the screen around them by at least 3. The ratio is the one of WCAG 2 (the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines): how much brighter the lighter of two colours is than the darker, from 1 for the same colour to 21 for black on white; 4.5 is its least for ordinary text, 3 its least for the edges of things on a screen. In the four looks the lowest ratio of letters is 7.8 (the kernel's letters in Night), and of frames 4.0 (Paper). The kernel does not check this itself; the looks are written into it and were checked from outside (see [testing.md](testing.md)). A look that broke the rule could leave the person at the machine unable to read their own screen.
- **The look in use** is what `look()` gives; every piece of the usual picture asks for it each time it paints. The kernel's lines do not (see "The kernel's lines" above). Every start begins with look 1.
- **`look_paint()` paints the whole screen again** in the look in use: the background and the sentence (`screen_background`), the chat box with everything in it (`chat_paint`), the panel with the text it showed last (`screen_panel_again`), the panel of apps, if any app is kept (`apps_panel_again`, see "The panel of apps" in [apps.md](apps.md)), and the frame with the table drawn last, if one was drawn since the start (`drawing_paint_again`, see "The table" above). Nothing else is on the screen. The background goes first, because it covers everything.
- **A look is picked** by the model, when the person asks for another look in the chat box (the tool `pick_look`, which keeps it at once; see [tools.md](tools.md)), or by the person at the question the onboarding asks once its questions about the key are over, kept with Enter alone; see "Picking a look" in [chat.md](chat.md). The look is kept in the notebook under the name `look`, as its name, such as `Night`; see [notebook.md](notebook.md).
- **At every later start** the kept look is put in use once the notebook is opened: `chat_begin` asks `look_begin` for it before it says the greeting. Nothing is painted then, because the kernel's lines cover the screen; the usual picture is first painted when the start is over, already in the kept look. A name the kernel does not know is never shown or used, and look 1 stays.
- **Only the startup code and the main loop may use `look.rs`:** the look in use and the state of the question for a look are plain `static mut` values that nothing protects.
- **The line `setup` brings the first look back** at once, whatever look was in use: `look_reset` puts look 1 in use and paints the whole screen again in it before the first question of `setup`, so that no look can leave the person at the machine unable to read the screen. When the notebook holds another look, an entry under `look` with no signs is kept at once, so that the next start shows look 1 too. See "Picking a look" in [chat.md](chat.md).
